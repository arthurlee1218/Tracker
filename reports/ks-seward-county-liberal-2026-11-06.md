# Liberal, Seward County, KS: Data Center Siting Brief

As of 2026-11-06 · Built from Data Center Watch entries and sentiment data, checked against public reporting · Not legal advice; confirm with local counsel

**Bottom line.** I found no named data center project, developer, application, moratorium or city or county vote on data centers in Liberal or Seward County, Kansas, and the tracker holds no entries naming Liberal.[^1] Data centers did become a campaign issue: at an Oct. 7 forum, county commission challengers Kay Burtzloff and Lizette Avalos-Morales said they oppose them over water, and both won on Nov. 3 (unofficial).[^2][^3] The Seward County Development Corporation's director, Eli Svaty, said in June that real projects are few and the Liberal City Commission would have wide room to negotiate if one came.[^4] The facts that will decide what happens next are the new commission's stance once seated, the county's electric capacity, and the state of groundwater rules in Groundwater Management District 3.[^2][^5] Statewide, the next shifts are the unsettled governor's race (recount request due Nov. 17) and the 2027 legislative session.[^6] "None found" is not proof that none exists: no city or county minutes were available to check.

## 1. Legal analysis

**Local status.** I could not confirm any vote, ordinance or moratorium on data centers by the Liberal City Commission or the Seward County Commission. The tracker lists none, and searches turned up only Seward County, Nebraska, which is a different place.[^1] The commission races above are the only recorded local political action. The City of Liberal and the county pages with agendas and minutes would not open for the researchers, so a Liberal or county action not covered by the news could exist.

**Zoning authority.** Inside Liberal, rezonings go to the Liberal Metropolitan Area Planning Commission (seven members, five city and two county, per a search summary of the city's page), which holds a hearing and recommends; the five-member City Commission decides.[^7] Under state law, notice runs at least 20 days before the hearing, the governing body can override the planning commission only by a two-thirds vote of its membership, and a protest petition from owners of 20% or more of the land forces a three-fourths vote.[^8] In unincorporated Seward County the county commission decides, and a search summary of K.S.A. 19-2960 says a protest requires a four-fifths vote.[^9] I could not read Liberal's zoning chapter or the county zoning regulations, so I cannot say whether a data center is a permitted, conditional or unlisted use.

**Citizen initiatives.** K.S.A. 12-3013 lets city voters propose an ordinance by petition: signatures equal to 25% (first-class cities) or 40% (second and third class) of electors who voted at the last regular city election. The governing body must adopt it unaltered within 20 days or call an election, and a majority of voters adopts it.[^10] Liberal's city class is not confirmed, so the threshold here is unknown. Administrative ordinances are excluded from the process.[^10]

**Water, incentives and disclosure.** SB 98 (2025) gives a qualified data center a 20-year state and local sales tax exemption. It requires at least $250 million of eligible costs within five years of operations starting, 20 full-time jobs within two years, a 10-year electricity purchase agreement and a water conservation plan. The Department of Commerce administers it, and the Kansas Intelligence Fusion Center and its oversight board review applicants. The Commerce page mentions no local role.[^11] I found no Kansas data-center-specific water or disclosure law. Water is governed by prior appropriation and permits from the Division of Water Resources, and Seward County lies in Groundwater Management District 3 (GMD 3).[^12]

**Outlook.** Two bills may return in 2027. SB 531 (2026) would bar large new data centers (10 MW or more) in counties with a recent drought emergency; it was referred to Senate Utilities in March 2026 with no later action found, and I know it only through another tracker, not independently confirmed.[^13] Democrats are projected to break the House Republican supermajority (68-57, unofficial), which would make vetoes easier to sustain.[^14]

**Does SB 98 limit a local ban or pause?** Ty Masterson (R), the governor nominee who voted for SB 98, says cities and counties should make siting decisions.[^15] I found no official, attorney general opinion or court that says SB 98 preempts local zoning, and the Commerce page describes only an incentive.[^11] I could not confirm the enrolled bill text, so I cannot rule out other language.

**Can voters ban data centers by petition?** This is live elsewhere in Kansas, not in Liberal. Emporia voters approved a 10-year ban on high-impact data centers, 4,346 to 2,891 (unofficial). Senior Judge Glen Braun had not ruled whether the petition is legislative or administrative, and the city says the ordinance cannot take effect until legal questions are resolved.[^16] Quinn Hughes, a Wichita attorney (Adam Jones Law Firm) for Emporia's petitioners, argues the test is whether an ordinance is primarily directed at executing a city's own goals; Michelle Stewart (Hinkle Law Firm), advising Ottawa's commission, said the petition's contents are mostly administrative. A Butler County judge held El Dorado's petition legislative.[^17] A ruling in Emporia, then any appeal, would settle the question for cities like Liberal.

## 2. What matters most here

**Water and the Ogallala.** Liberal draws from the High Plains (Ogallala) aquifer through 21 city-owned wells over 200 feet deep, with four ground-level reservoirs and a 1,000,000-gallon tower. The city page gives no daily use or capacity.[^18] GMD 3's first district-wide conservation plan has been rejected twice by Earl Lewis, chief engineer of the Division of Water Resources, and GMD 3 will appeal to the Secretary of Agriculture; Trevor Ahring, the district's civil engineer, wrote it.[^19] A search summary reports a 35% cut target for southern Stevens and Seward counties, which I could not verify on an opened page.[^20] Svaty says state safeguards would keep high-water-use projects out of western Kansas and local projects would likely use liquid or air cooling; the commissioners-elect disagree on risk.[^4][^2]

**Power supply.** Liberal's electricity comes from Southern Pioneer Electric, not the city.[^21] Per a search summary, it buys wholesale power from Sunflower Electric, a western Kansas generation cooperative; I could not open Sunflower's own pages to confirm.[^22] Sunflower's economic development page says it assesses large new loads with its member utilities but lists no thresholds or fees.[^23] At the forum, District 4 challenger Jose Lara said "We only have 20 megawatts available to us" and that substations cannot exceed 8 MW. The paper did not verify this, and the two figures appear to conflict.[^2] The KCC's Evergy large-load tariff (75 MW and up, 12-year minimum, 80% minimum bill) covers Evergy's territory, and I found no evidence it covers Liberal.[^24] SB 348 (effective March 19, 2026) lets Southern Pioneer's board set rates without a KCC rate case, per search summaries of Legislature pages, so I found no state review step for a Liberal large-load contract.[^25]

**Local revenue pitch.** Svaty put power franchise fees at about $2.5 million a year per 100 MW of data center load and said the city has "a large amount of leeway" in dealing with companies. His other prospects are not data centers; "Project Pillars" makes equipment enclosures for them and has a state incentive package.[^4]

**Wastewater.** Liberal's new treatment plant is designed for 4.05 million gallons per day, and the commission approved a $10 monthly sewer improvement fee from April 1, 2025 because the wastewater fund faced a negative cash balance within three years.[^26][^27] I did not find the plant's permit limits.

## 4. Public sentiment

**What the data shows.** The tracker holds 0 saved social posts for Liberal, so there is no local online reading.[^30] Statewide it holds 124 Kansas posts: 96 against, 22 neutral, 5 mixed, 1 supportive. These are people who post online, not residents, and they are mostly about Emporia, El Dorado and the governor's race.[^30]

**The main concerns, explained.** For Liberal, the one concern in local coverage is water. Burtzloff said data centers are "a tremendous user of water," and Avalos-Morales said "We are at risk of losing water."[^2] Vazquez, an incumbent, said "If it affects our water, I will say no to that," but also called data centers expensive and "probably not coming here."[^2] Statewide, water was raised in 19 posts (12 against, 0 supportive, 4 mixed), alongside land (29), tech (21), power (20) and rates (19).[^30] The Kansas Speaks survey, from Fort Hays State University's Docking Institute, found a majority "somewhat or deeply concerned" about data centers' energy and water use, though the article gives no sample size or dates.[^31]

**Voices on each side.** No Liberal posts exist, so I name none. The most-engaged Kansas post is on X from @JasonBassler1 on July 26, 2026 (38,701 likes by the tracker's count) about an Emporia zoning meeting; the tracker records 4 replies against and 0 supportive. The page would not open (HTTP 402), so I cannot say what the account's profile states.[^30] The other top posts are statewide and against; I did not open them. I found no saved supportive post for Kansas beyond one in the tracker's counts, and none from Liberal. The strongest supportive local argument is Svaty's: franchise fees and economic development, with water limited by cooling design and state checks.[^4] Lara's position sits between: against hyperscale sites, "But smaller ones perhaps."[^2]

**What offline signals add.** On Nov. 3, Burtzloff beat Louderback 369-216 in District 2 and Avalos-Morales beat Vazquez 533-250 in District 3; Bloom, the District 4 incumbent, beat Lara 445-403. Turnout was 4,223 of 11,615 registered voters (36.36%).[^3] The results story does not mention data centers, and no poll of Liberal or Seward County exists that I found, so it is not established that data centers decided either race.[^3] In the governor's race, Seward County voted Cindy Holscher 2,267 and Ty Masterson 1,898; Holscher backs a pause on new approvals.[^3][^6]

## 5. Other good-to-knows

- Do not confuse this place with Seward County, Nebraska, which passed a one-year pause in June 2026, or Texas County, Oklahoma.[^1]
- Kansas counties elsewhere have acted: Harvey County adopted a moratorium on Jan. 13, 2026, through the end of 2028, with no application filed.[^32]
- Sedgwick County extended its pause on Sept. 2 for three more months, to about Dec. 24, 2026.[^33]
- Dates to watch: the Nov. 17 recount request deadline in the governor's race, and the Emporia court ruling on the citizen petition.[^6][^16]
- The county has a candidate-claimed 20 MW of available power; a utility figure would be the key fact if a developer appears.[^2]
