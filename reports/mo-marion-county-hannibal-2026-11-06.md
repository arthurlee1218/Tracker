# Hannibal, Marion County, MO: Data Center Siting Brief

As of 2026-11-06 · Built from Data Center Watch entries and sentiment data, checked against public reporting · Not legal advice; confirm with local counsel

**Bottom line.** Hannibal has no named data center developer, no application and no council vote. In late July 2026 the city said several companies had made unsolicited inquiries about buying land, that none had been chosen, and that the council had not discussed the matter in open or closed session.[^1][^2] Separately, the city-owned Board of Public Works (HBPW) offered up to 100 MW of retail power at its Lakeside Technology Park through an RFP whose proposals were due May 15, 2026; I could not confirm whether any arrived.[^3] The main legal question is how much Hannibal can regulate on its own: I found no Missouri statute that blocks a city pause or ban, but also no Hannibal rule written for data centers, and a Benton County judge has preliminarily blocked a county moratorium.[^4][^5] Local feeling online is strongly against (32 of 41 saved Facebook posts), and 68% of likely Missouri voters oppose a data center in their community in a statewide poll.[^6][^7] The next visible step is the staff report the city promised to present to council in open session, which has no date.[^2]

## 1. Legal analysis

**Local status.** Hannibal has taken no vote on data centers that I could find. The city statement says no company was selected, no formal proposal went to council, and staff and HBPW and the Hannibal Regional Economic Development Council (HREDC) are in a "fact-finding phase" and favor an RFP over negotiating with one company.[^1][^2] The city says that if a project advances, council will hold public meetings and hearings and take resident input before deciding.[^2] The vote tallies and who introduced items that the brief template asks for do not exist at the city level yet. I could not open any Hannibal council agenda or minutes; the document download found none.

**Zoning authority.** City Attorney James Lemon, who represents both Hannibal and Palmyra, said the two cities are not bound by county zoning.[^4] Hannibal's zoning is Chapter 13 of its code, and the council must refer a change to the planning and zoning commission first; I could not open the code text (the page returned an error) and found no data-center-specific use or definition, so whether Lakeside's industrial zoning permits a data center by right is unconfirmed.[^37] HBPW's April minutes (seen only in a search summary) describe the 106-acre city-owned Lakeside site as having industrial zoning, power, water, sewer and 100 Gbps fiber.[^6a]

**County action does not reach the city.** Marion County commissioners adopted a 12-month moratorium on new or expanded data centers on June 15, 2026, on the Planning and Zoning Commission's recommendation; the tracker says the vote was unanimous.[^24] Presiding Commissioner David Lomax said on July 14 that the county lacks infrastructure for large data centers.[^4] At the July 13 meeting Susan Gard asked for a volunteer task force, saying she was neither for nor against, and the commission approved it.[^22] The tracker logs that on July 27 the county lifted the moratorium, adopted Land Use Order revisions and formed the task force.[^23] That conflicts with WGEM's August 21 report in which Palmyra Mayor Rusty Adrian described a one-year county moratorium as in effect.[^25] I could not open the July 27 minutes or the amended order, so I cannot say which is right. Either way the county order covers unincorporated land, so it does not govern Hannibal, a conclusion that rests on Lemon's statement and not on the order's text.[^4]

**Citizen initiatives.** I could not confirm Hannibal's charter rules on local initiative or referendum; searches returned only the statewide constitutional provision. Ralls County, next door, is the only nearby government I found planning a public vote, possibly in April.[^26]

**Water, incentives and disclosure.** The state sales and use tax exemption in RSMo 144.810 requires at least $25 million of investment within up to 36 months and at least 10 new jobs at 150% of the county average wage for a new facility ($5 million and 5 jobs for an expansion), for up to 15 years (new) or 10 (expansion). The Department of Economic Development certifies and the agreement must set "repayment penalties."[^20] I found no Missouri law requiring data center water-use disclosure; a Department of Natural Resources (DNR) page says its report to the Governor's Office is due November 30, 2026.[^21] The city's own estimate is that a small data center could add "tens of millions" of dollars a year in tax revenue, much of it to Hannibal Public Schools; that is a claim by the city, not an independent forecast.[^2]

**Outlook.** No data center bill passed in 2026; Speaker Jon Patterson called it a local issue.[^19] Rep. Colin Wellenkamp's HB 3362 (grid-cost, water-permit and curtailment rules) got a hearing but no floor vote and is expected back in 2027.[^19][^36] Gov. Mike Kehoe refused a special session, saying SB 4 already protects ratepayers and siting is local.[^34] The November 3 election ended the Republican supermajorities in both chambers by unofficial counts, which makes veto overrides harder though Republicans keep majorities.[^35]

**Can a Missouri city or county pause data centers without a specific statute?** This is the live question, but it has been argued over counties, not cities. *Case that limits local power:* in Benton County, a judge issued a preliminary injunction on October 6, 2026 stopping the county from enforcing its moratorium against developer NSI 6 LLC, reportedly finding the ordinance did not identify a specific public health threat; that is from a search summary, since the article returned an error.[^28] County attorney Travis Elliott defended the moratorium, arguing it applied because developer Trent Overhue did not yet own the land, and Overhue chose Benton partly because it has no planning and zoning.[^27] Webster County's own notice said its legal theory might rest on nuisance abatement (RSMo 49.950(3)) and that without planning and zoning its authority "may be more limited."[^29] *Case that allows it:* St. Charles County's counsel reportedly advised that Missouri case law supports a six-month moratorium; this comes only from an AI-summary service, so treat it as a lead.[^38] *Unsettled:* whether a moratorium needs a stated health or safety basis, and how long one may run. A final Benton judgment, an appeal or an Attorney General opinion would settle it; I found no opinion. Hannibal has its own zoning chapter, so its position differs from a county with none, but no court has ruled on a city.

## 2. What matters most here

**A municipal utility that sets its own terms.** Power in Hannibal comes from HBPW, which the city owns. It buys 20 MW from the Prairie State Energy Campus (in use since June 1, 2017), buys the rest in the MISO day-ahead market, and has a summer peak of about 55 MW, so a 100 MW offer is nearly double the system's peak load.[^7a][^8] Council approved a 40-year agreement for the Northeast Generation project (first phase 79 MW, Hannibal share about 30 MW, target 2029), and sold a 12-acre Lakeside lot for it; both are known only from search summaries of Muddy River News.[^10][^11] HBPW's rate page lists a Large Industrial Service schedule (1,000 kW and up) but no large-load or data center tariff.[^12] Missouri's SB 4 large-load tariffs were approved for investor-owned Ameren and Evergy; whether they reach a city utility was not verified, and my reading that they do not is an inference.[^18]

**Who controls the utility.** Rate anger has become a governance fight. HBPW approved a 7% electric increase unanimously after a February 17 hearing, effective March 4, the third since mid-2024; it tied the rise to roughly $1.7 million of extra purchased-power cost from January's winter storm.[^12][^13] 4th Ward Alderman Euhlan Leeders said ratepayers were being "jacked way up," 2nd Ward Alderwoman April Azotea said the board could cut costs, and board president John Ortwerth said power prices swing.[^13] Mayor Darrell McCoy said council wants HBPW to act as "an executive department" of the city and noted that Ortwerth's term expires in June 2026.[^14] Who has the final say on a large-load contract is unconfirmed; the board issued the RFP, while council has approved some power contracts.[^5][^9]

**Water and wastewater capacity.** HBPW's water plant has a finished-water capacity of 7.5 million gallons a day; a 2021 report put average use near 3.2 million.[^15] The wastewater plant is rated at 6 million gallons a day and averages about 3, discharging to the Mississippi River under DNR permit MO-0093513, per a search summary; nutrient limits reportedly tighten in 2030.[^16][^17] No source I found gives a data center's likely water demand, and none names the water intake. A 2 million gallon figure in HB 3362 was proposed as a state permit trigger, not a Hannibal limit.[^19]

**Rate and tax claims are contested but unproven.** Residents link data centers to higher bills, but the documented 2024 to 2026 increases were attributed to purchased-power costs, not a data center, since none exists.[^13] On the other side, the city's tax estimate has no project behind it.[^2] Mayor McCoy told KHQA he had not seen a request for proposals and did not know its details, while HBPW and HREDC materials describe one issued in March or April.[^3a][^5]

## 4. Public sentiment

**What the data shows.** The tracker holds 41 saved Facebook posts for Hannibal, May 1 to November 4, 2026: 32 against, 7 supportive, 2 mixed, with no other platform. They are people who post online, not residents, and nearly all are comments under one July 30 thread.[^p]

**The main concerns, explained.**
- *Water (10 posts: 8 against, 2 supportive).* Opponents write that they have "read articles" and do not want a data center; one supportive commenter says recirculating systems resolved water issues. That claim is unverified, and no Hannibal project exists to measure.[^c1][^c2]
- *Rates (8 posts: 7 against, 1 mixed).* One commenter says HBPW "just raised the price on electric" because it is costly to produce. The increase is real, but HBPW attributes it to purchased-power costs.[^c3][^13]
- *Power (6: 4 against, 1 supportive, 1 mixed) and land (5: 4 against, 1 mixed).* These tie to the 100 MW offer at Lakeside and to the eminent domain worry Lemon addressed, saying Hannibal would not cooperate with anyone seeking residents' land.[^p][^4]
- *Jobs and tax (3 and 2 posts, split).* Supporters write that data centers create jobs; the HBPW minutes summary lists limited permanent jobs as a concern.[^c4][^6a]

**Voices on each side.** Account names do not load without logging in, so names are not visible without logging in; all saved accounts are individuals, and I did not look anyone up.
- *Against:* the July 30 thread "Data Centers in Hannibal Here are the FACTS and a few curious questions" in Facebook group 646673738690377 (page title "Things Happening In Hannibal, MO"), engagement 123/210, top 1% on the platform; replies read 32 against, 6 supportive, 1 mixed.[^31] A May 15 post, "Do you all know this is also going on in our home town!!", in group 1096582393715845, engagement 33/27; replies 3 against, 1 supportive, 1 mixed.[^32] A July 30 comment asking "who is the one for it" (22/8).[^c5]
- *Supportive:* a July 30 comment arguing data centers are needed given reliance on online services (2/22);[^c6] "Data Centers creates JOBS!" (2/12);[^c4] and a comment doubting that critics "actually know" the facts (2/7).[^c7] Replies under these are not stored individually.

**What offline signals add.** The SLU/YouGov poll of 900 likely voters (August 13 to 24, ±4.09%) found 68% oppose a data center in their community and 17% support, up 17 points since February; there is no breakdown for northeast Missouri.[^7] The only named speakers at a public meeting I found are Susan Gard at the county (neutral) and Palmyra's more than 30 unnamed residents.[^22][^25] The strongest version of the other side: the city says unsolicited buyers exist, wants to be prepared, and sees tax revenue worth examining, and one Hannibal-specific mixed comment says the town needs a Highway 61 bypass first.[^3a][^c8] No organized Hannibal group, petition or Hannibal poll turned up.

## 5. Other good-to-knows

- Lakeside Technology Park is city-owned. In September 2024 the city received a $1.71 million offer for all 95 acres from Four Points Land Surveying & Engineering (president Norman Ellerbrock) for offices or light industry, not a data center.[^33]
- The city statement is the only on-record sign of buyers; Mayor McCoy and the HBPW materials are not aligned on the RFP, so a Sunshine Law request to HBPW and the city for RFP responses is the clearest route to the facts. I could not confirm responses exist.[^3a][^5]
- Watch DNR's report due November 30, 2026, and the 2027 return of HB 3362.[^21][^19]
- Neighbors are acting: Ralls County's moratorium and possible April vote, Palmyra's task force and petition, and the Benton County injunction as a test of moratorium law.[^26][^25][^28]
- Rep. Tricia Byrnes' planned transparency hearing was canceled so it would not compete with a Capitol rally; her bill would require public review of hyperscale projects.[^39]
