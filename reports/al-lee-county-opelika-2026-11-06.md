**Bottom line.** I found no named data center project, developer, rezoning, abatement or utility request in Opelika. The one project residents tied to data centers, Faith Technologies' $79.25 million plant on Anderson Road, is described by the city and the company as manufacturing, and the council approved it unanimously on Feb. 24, 2026.[^1] Residents asked the council for data center limits in 11 public comments at four meetings from May 5 to June 16, and the council took no action in that period.[^3] In late October, Facebook posts claimed "multiple data centers" were coming; no source for that claim is public, and I found no city statement on it.[^21][^22] The biggest legal question is practical: Opelika's only moratorium covers residential building, not data centers, and I could not confirm whether the zoning ordinance names data centers at all.[^5][^6] The state's new 150 MW contract-review law applies to data centers in Alabama Power's service territory, and Opelika buys power through its own city utility, so it does not reach a project here.[^11][^12][^14] What changes next: the planning staff's zoning rewrite is due before the Planning Commission in February or March 2027.[^6]

## 1. Legal analysis

**Local status.** There is no data center moratorium or ordinance in Opelika that I could find. The council approved a 12-month planning moratorium on new residential and multiple-occupancy development 4–1 on Feb. 17, 2026; it runs from May 1, 2026 to April 30, 2027.[^5] Ward 5 council member Todd Rauch's amendments on accountability and reporting failed 4–1, and Ward 4 council member Chuck Beams said he was "completely for the moratorium." The article does not say who cast the dissenting vote or who introduced it.[^5] City Attorney Robbie Treese is named in that coverage.[^5] A City of Opelika Facebook post titled "The Opelika City Council will consider a proposed one-year planning moratorium" came up in a search, but only the title loaded, so I cannot say it relates to anything beyond this residential pause.[^31] On data centers specifically, the tracker's council-minute data log 11 public comments: three on May 5, three on May 19, three on June 2 and two on June 16, asking for a moratorium, conditional-use permits or reporting rules; the council took no action.[^3][^4] (The tracker's summary entry says "four residents" and "five meetings"; its own minute data show more.) I could not open the minutes, so there are no roll calls, speaker names or motions for those meetings. The council's Nov. 3 meeting had no data center item; it introduced a rezoning of about 18.9 acres on Andrews Road from M-2 industrial to C-3 commercial.[^37]

**Zoning authority.** Alabama follows Dillon's Rule: local governments hold only the powers the state grants. Incorporated cities such as Opelika hold zoning and planning power, while most counties do not.[^8] Lee County is one of the few exceptions: a 2007 local act (Act 2007-401) created a county planning commission whose zoning applies in any unincorporated beat where a majority of voters opt in, as Beat 13 (around Beulah) did in 2021. In zoned beats, heavy industrial uses need a Special District approved by the planning commission and the county commission; the rest of unincorporated Lee County is unzoned.[^38] Association of County Commissioners of Alabama executive director Sonny Brasfield said commissioners cannot assume any power not specifically granted by the Legislature.[^8] In Opelika, the Planning Commission hears rezonings and conditional uses and recommends to the council.[^32] Planning Director Matt Mosley told the council at its Oct. 20 work session that staff are rewriting parts of the zoning ordinance, including permitted uses by district and the line between light and heavy manufacturing. Changes could reach the Planning Commission, with a council recommendation in February or March 2027. The article does not mention data centers.[^6] I could not read Opelika's zoning ordinance text (the PDF would not decode), so I could not confirm whether a data center is a permitted, conditional or unlisted use.

**Citizen initiatives.** Alabama has no statewide initiative or referendum. A Ballotpedia summary says only Birmingham has a municipal initiative and referendum process, and Opelika is not listed; I could not open the page itself, so treat this as unverified.[^33]

**Water, incentives and disclosure.** HB 399 caps new data center abatements at 20 years for abatements granted from Jan. 1, 2027, with up to 10 more years if the operator signs a binding local-benefits agreement. Sites of 100 MW or more pay state sales and non-educational property taxes once in service.[^13] I found no Alabama rule that requires data centers to disclose water use. A state registration requirement for withdrawals of 100,000 gallons a day or more exists but is not specific to data centers; I saw it only in a search summary.[^34]

**Outlook.** Counties are the level in debate: the county commissioners' association voted unanimously in August against zoning power limited to data centers and asked for a broader review of county planning authority.[^10] Sen. Arthur Orr (R-Decatur) said he drafted a Morgan County local bill for next spring.[^8] I found no Lee County bill.

**Does Act 2026-610 reach a city-owned utility?** No. The Act requires the Public Service Commission to find that contracts for data centers needing 150 MW or more recover incremental costs and benefit other customers, and it applies to data centers in Alabama Power's service territory.[^11] The procedures the PSC adopted on Oct. 6 govern contracts between Alabama Power and those data centers: a public summary petition, a confidential full contract for PSC staff and the Attorney General, and a vote within 90 days or the contract is deemed approved. Commissioners Chris Beeker and Jeremy Oden argued against letting contracts lapse for lack of a vote; the default-approval clause was the compromise.[^12] Opelika's power comes from Opelika Power Services, which the city runs and the council prices, so a data center here would negotiate with the city and its wholesale supplier, not the PSC.[^14][^16] The related dispute is over the 90-day rule: Beeker says it prompts timely action, while SELC and Energy Alabama's Daniel Tait say silence still counts as approval.[^12]

## 2. What matters most here

**A rumor with no filing behind it.** The "multiple data centers" claim began circulating Oct. 27 in a Facebook group and was repeated Oct. 30 in another.[^21][^22] Neither names a developer, site or timeline. The tracker holds no project for Opelika.[^3] Nearby, in Auburn, Core Scientific holds a permit for a $55 million expansion of its existing data center on Samford Avenue (staff from 11 to at least 20), which residents may be reading across.[^2]

**Power comes through a city utility with a wholesale supplier.** Opelika Power Services is a city-run utility at 600 Fox Run Parkway and is one of 11 members of the Alabama Municipal Electric Authority, branded Energy Southeast.[^14][^15] The city council sets electric rates by ordinance; a September 2025 first reading would raise the monthly customer charge to $23.50 in 2026 and lower the per-kWh energy charge, and City Attorney Guy Gunter said the average bill should not rise.[^16] I could not confirm final adoption. Customer count (about 16,000) comes only from aggregator figures I could not open. I found no large-load tariff and no data on substations or peak load.

**Water has a drought and a neighbor.** Opelika draws on two surface sources: Saugahatchee Lake and Halawakee Creek on Lake Harding.[^17] The Saugahatchee plant makes 8 million gallons a day (2013 figure).[^18] In 2016, demand was 5 to 7 million gallons a day, Opelika sells water to Auburn, Beauregard, Beulah and Loachapoka, and then-utility manager Dan Hilyer called Lake Harding "very secure."[^19] I found no current use figure. Auburn Water Works Board declared a Phase II drought warning in April 2026, its first since 2016, and Auburn has bought more water from Opelika (from a search summary; the page did not open).[^35] Opelika's Water Works Board members are Eric Canda, Jeffery Hilyer, Bill Brown, Regina Meadows and George Allen.[^17]

**Wastewater capacity is modest and mostly residential.** The Westside plant is rated 4.0 million gallons a day and the Eastside plant 5 million; the system serves more than 13,500 customers, about 96% residential.[^20] Council approved about $600,000 in emergency repairs to the Northeast Industrial Park Lift Station 16 force main in September 2025, in the same park where Faith Technologies sits.[^16] The October 2026 update mentions a 12-inch force main from the park to the east side plant.[^6] I could not read permit limits.

## 4. Public sentiment

**What the data shows.** The tracker's sample for Opelika (May 1 to Nov. 4, 2026) holds 47 posts: 36 on Facebook and 11 from council-minute public comment. By stance: 29 against, 15 supportive, 2 mixed, 1 neutral. These are people who post online, not residents, and engagement reflects reach, not agreement. Nearly all the Facebook posts come from one thread.[^21]

**The main concerns, explained.**

*Water (7 posts: 3 against, 2 supportive, 1 mixed).* Speakers at council meetings cited water, and one cited a Bessemer site using about 2 million gallons a day.[^3] That is another city's figure, not Opelika's. Opelika's current water use and any data center demand are unknown (see section 2).

*Rates (7: 3 against, 2 supportive, 1 mixed).* One commenter wrote "we can expect our power bills to go up."[^23] Opelika's rates are set by its own council and supplier, so the state's PSC process does not govern them (section 1). A supportive commenter said demand drives the buildout.[^23]

*Jobs (7: 2 against, 3 supportive, 1 mixed).* Supporters asked "Why would you want to stop jobs."[^23] The real figures on the table are FTI's 200 jobs, which officials say are not data center jobs.[^1]

*Power (5: 2, 2, 1).* Both sides cite grid demand. No Opelika utility figures exist in the record I found.

**Voices on each side.** Facebook shows commenter names on the page, but the tracker does not store them, and the pages I could open showed no comments, so I cannot give individual account names ("name not visible without logging in").

- *Against:* An individual in the group "Beauregard Community Concerns" posted on Oct. 27 that rumors of "MULTIPLE DATA CENTERS" were circulating; 176 reactions and 172 comments, replies read 17 against, 16 supportive, 2 mixed (tracker count; I could not verify the comments).[^21] Another individual, in the group "Alabama Citizens Against Data Centers" (tagline "Protecting Alabama's Future"), posted Oct. 30 that they had heard several were planned; 18 reactions, 16 comments.[^22] Both rest on rumor, and I could not find who runs either group.
- *Supportive:* An individual commented Oct. 27, "Looking forward to them. Great for our area!" (12 reactions, 6 comments). Another, the same day, argued that people complaining on Facebook rely on the same data centers (22 reactions, 6 comments).[^23] Neither account's profile is visible without logging in.

**What offline signals add.** The February hearing on FTI drew residents Jason Manns (downtown resident, who said agenda materials lacked detail and there was no cost-benefit analysis), Morgan Carpenter (resident since 1972, who said more transparency would have avoided the confusion) and Mike Bass (who asked the council to table the vote). Some asked whether officials had signed non-disclosure agreements and whether the project could lead to a data center.[^1] Names of the May to June speakers are not available to me. Statewide, a Cygnal poll of 500 likely Republican primary voters on April 29–30 found 22% saying data centers are good for Alabama, 25% bad, 37% a mix, 16% unsure; there is no Lee County breakout.[^24] Governor-elect Tommy Tuberville (unofficial, about 52–48 over Doug Jones) said in October that communities will decide where data centers are built, after earlier urging Alabamians to welcome them; Jones had backed a moratorium.[^25][^28][^39] The strongest version of the supportive side is the jobs and tax base argument the mayor makes for FTI.[^1][^7]

## 5. Other good-to-knows

- Cities nearby in Alabama have acted: Cullman passed a 12-month moratorium in June 2026, and Foley approved a six-month moratorium Sept. 8, 2026, covering data centers, crypto mining and server farms.[^9][^26]
- Most Alabama counties cannot act, but Lee County can zone in beats that voted for it; land in unzoned beats outside Opelika and Auburn has no land-use regulator.[^10][^38]
- Alabama Power data center contracts have been filed with key terms redacted, and the PSC and Attorney General's office get unredacted copies.[^11]
- To watch: the moratorium on residential building expires April 30, 2027, and the council can extend or shorten it; the zoning rewrite reaches the Planning Commission in February or March 2027; any rezoning or abatement agenda item naming a data center would settle the rumor.[^5][^6]
- I could not confirm any official response to the October rumors, any Opelika Power Services or chamber of commerce statement, or the council minutes behind the tracker's entry.
