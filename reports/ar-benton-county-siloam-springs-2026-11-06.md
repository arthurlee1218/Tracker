# Siloam Springs, Benton County, AR: Data Center Siting Brief

As of 2026-11-06 · Built from Data Center Watch entries and sentiment data, checked against public reporting · Not legal advice; confirm with local counsel

**Bottom line.** Siloam Springs has no data center application on record, but two developers were in talks with the city when the board began a six-month moratorium. The Board of Directors passed Ordinance 26-25 on first reading Oct. 6 (4-1) and second reading Oct. 20 (5-2)[^1][^2]. The tracker records final adoption on Nov. 3 by 5-2, but I could not confirm that from an official record[^3]. The biggest legal question is whether Arkansas Act 851 of 2023, written mostly for crypto mining, stops a city from pausing or banning data centers; it has not been tested in an Arkansas court[^4]. The city owns its electric utility and buys power from the Grand River Dam Authority, and its wastewater plant is a stated concern[^5][^6]. The pause bars new approvals and special utility deals for six months, during which the City Administrator must review codes and utility capacity[^7]. The next real decision is whether the board extends or ends the pause, or adopts data center rules, around May 2027 if the Nov. 3 vote stands.

## 1. Legal analysis

**Local status.** Director Lesa Rissler introduced Ordinance 26-25[^7]. On Oct. 6 the board passed the first reading 4-1; Brad Burns voted no and David Allen and Mindy Hunt were absent[^1]. Rissler said, "This isn't a permanent moratorium, it's only six months."[^1] On Oct. 20 the second reading passed 5-2. Ken Wiles, Blair, Rissler, Allen and Hunt voted yes; Carroll and Burns voted no (first names for Blair and Carroll are not in the sources I read). Allen moved and Rissler seconded[^2]. The Nov. 3 agenda lists the ordinance for third reading[^8]. The tracker says it passed 5-2 that day, citing a Facebook page, "Siloam Springs Citizen's Page"[^3]. The Nov. 3 minutes were not available to me, so I could not confirm the roll call or the effective date.

**What the ordinance does.** It bars the city from accepting or approving rezoning, site plans, building permits or other approvals for a new data center. It also bars utility agreements, special rate deals and capacity reservations "specifically for" one[^7]. Existing data centers, and any with all final approvals and permits issued before the effective date, are exempt. The pause lasts six months from the effective date, and an extension needs further board action. An emergency clause makes it effective on passage[^7]. The City Administrator, Scot Rigby, must review the Unified Development Code and utility capacity, take public input and recommend rules; any code change goes to the Planning Commission and then the board[^7]. The definition has no size or megawatt threshold[^7].

**Zoning authority.** The city board adopts zoning by ordinance after a Planning Commission hearing, with notice by posted sign, mailed letters and a legal notice. Applicants must "convincingly demonstrate" six findings[^9]. The city's code has no standards for large data centers[^7]. The county is not shown to have a role inside city limits; I did not find a Benton County Quorum Court action on data centers.

**Water, incentives and disclosure.** Act 548 of 2025 lowered the state sales and use tax exemption threshold for a Qualified Data Center from $500 million to $100 million over five years, with a $1 million compensation requirement. It added a "Qualified Large Data Center" tier at $2 billion[^10]. I found no state water or disclosure rule for data centers. State ratepayer scrutiny so far concerns Entergy and a Google-linked solar rider in West Memphis, which does not serve Siloam Springs[^11].

**Does Act 851 limit a local pause or ban?** Act 851 (HB1799) is titled the "Arkansas Data Centers Act of 2023" and was enacted to "clarify the regulation of the digital asset mining business."[^12] A search summary of an Association of Arkansas Counties page says it added Ark. Code 14-1-505, barring local rules that discriminate against crypto mining. I could not open that page or the act text, and reports conflict on whether 2024 acts changed it[^12]. The Democrat-Gazette's local story says Act 851 bars cities from banning data centers outright but leaves zoning and operating standards to them[^1].

- *The case that it limits cities.* Little Rock City Attorney Tom Carpenter said the act's title and references "could be enough for a judge" to rule against a locality that bans data centers[^4]. Rep. Josh Bryant (R-Rogers), a co-sponsor, said the intent was to prevent discrimination against both data centers and crypto mining, and that a data center ban should be left to the state[^4]. Attorney General Tim Griffin's Nov. 2023 opinion said a city or county "cannot prevent a new digital asset mining operation," which concerns crypto[^4].
- *The case that it does not.* John Wilkerson, general counsel of the Arkansas Municipal League, said, "I think that Act 851 dealt with crypto."[^4] Sen. Rick McClure (R-Malvern), the lead sponsor, was unsure the act covers new data centers[^4]. City attorney Heather Owens of Harrington Miller Law Firm told the board on Oct. 6 it has authority to pass the ordinance[^1].
- *What is unsettled and what would settle it.* The Attorney General's office said it will not say whether Act 851 covers hyperscale data centers without a formal opinion request, and the article reports no Arkansas lawsuit[^4]. A formal opinion, a court ruling or an amendment would settle it. Ordinance Section 7 says it applies "consistently with applicable state and federal law," which hedges the question[^7]. I found no named challenger to Siloam Springs' ordinance.

**Citizen initiatives.** I could not confirm Arkansas's initiative and referendum rules as they apply to a city ordinance, and found no petition on this ordinance.

**Outlook.** A non-binding interim resolution (2025-008) by Sen. Fred Love, the Democratic nominee for governor, asks for a statewide moratorium while the state writes standards. The Arkansas Legislative Council sent it to committees[^13]. The ordinance cites it[^7]. Gov. Sarah Huckabee Sanders has not moved to restrict data centers; spokesperson Sam Dubke cited clean water and lower costs[^13]. I found no bills filed for 2027.

## 2. What matters most here

**Power: a city-owned utility buying from GRDA.** Siloam Springs runs its own electric department under Director Phil Stokes, with 9,351 customers, four substations and 10 miles of 69 kV line (December 2024)[^5]. It buys wholesale power from the Grand River Dam Authority under a 1989 contract that Stokes said ends in 2042[^14]. The grid operator is Southwest Power Pool; in February 2021 an energy emergency led to rotating outages of about 45 minutes each[^15]. I could not find peak load, spare capacity or a large-load tariff. A Citizen Portal summary (an aggregator, not opened) says a commissioner reported prospective customers seeking allocations far larger than current industrial users, and that GRDA may ask for contract extensions; I could not confirm it[^16].

**Wastewater capacity and a bond vote.** The wastewater plant is designed for 5.3 million gallons a day and discharges to Sager Creek[^17]. Utility Commission member Bernadette Keck raised the wastewater upgrades a data center would need[^1]. The Nov. 3 ballot asked voters to renew a 5/8-cent sales tax and approve $55 million in bonds for wastewater work; Public Works Director Josh Napier is the city contact. I did not find the result[^18]. The state environmental agency's file shows the plant's discharge permit renewal was received late on Oct. 15, 2026[^19].

**Water supply.** Raw water comes from the Illinois River in Oklahoma, stored in three reservoirs; the system serves about 18,000 people[^20]. A roughly $30 million plant and intake upgrade was finished in August 2022[^21]. I could not find the plant's post-upgrade capacity. Mavria said it would use closed-loop cooling and bring its own water to start, but did not know its total need[^1].

**Rates already rising.** The board approved a rate ordinance 6-0 on Dec. 16, 2025: electric 0%, water 4% and wastewater 4% in 2026, then 4% a year for each in 2027 to 2030. The average resident pays about $8 more a month in 2026[^22]. Rates are the second most common concern in local posts (see section 4).

## 4. Public sentiment

**What the data shows.** The tracker holds 65 saved posts for Siloam Springs from May 1 to Nov. 4, 2026: 64 Facebook comments and one council-minute entry. By stance, 53 are against, 6 supportive, 4 mixed and 2 neutral. These are people who post online, not residents, and engagement shows reach, not agreement[^3]. Statewide the tracker holds 274 Arkansas posts, 247 against[^3].

**The main concerns, explained.**

- *Water (16 posts: 15 against, 1 mixed).* Commenters worry about supply and contamination. One comment in a Facebook group on Oct. 19 asked about "water usage and contamination," with 30 reactions and 5 replies[^25]. This connects to the unknown water need of the two proposals and to Keck's wastewater comment[^1].
- *Rates (9 posts: 8 against, 1 mixed).* A comment on Oct. 19 warned that costs will rise "if you think stuff is expensive now"[^25]. This sits beside the scheduled 4% yearly rate increases. The ordinance bars special rate deals for data centers during the pause[^7][^22].
- *Noise (8 posts, all against).* The ordinance lists noise and backup generators among the topics the city must review[^7].
- *Tech and land (7 each).* Land has one supportive post. Six more posts focus on the pause itself, five of them against, such as the comment "Six-month moratorium isn't long enough."[^26]

**Voices on each side.** The tracker does not store commenter names. All are individual accounts, and names are not visible without opening the pages, which did not load for the researchers[^3]. I do not give names or look anyone up. How replies read could not be checked.

- Against: a comment, "I'd rather have a Costco than a data center," on the Arkansas Democrat-Gazette's Facebook page, Oct. 10, 2026 (45 reactions, 2 replies, top 4% on its platform)[^26]. A comment on the same thread, "Six-month moratorium isn't long enough," had 34 reactions[^26]. A water-contamination comment in a Facebook group (ID 2131183733833006, name and organizer not visible), Oct. 19, had 30 reactions and 5 replies[^25].
- Supportive: "Everyone who reacted on this used a data center," Oct. 10, on the Democrat-Gazette's page (6 reactions, 9 replies)[^26]. A tax-focused comment urged building it and taxing it (9 reactions, 6 replies)[^26]. A jobs-and-land comment in the Oct. 19 group thread said it would bring jobs (1 reaction, 3 replies)[^25].
- Mixed: a comment that any project "should be capable of co-generating the power it requires" (5 reactions), posted Oct. 6 on a Facebook page permalink that is the same page ID as "Siloam Springs Citizen's Page" in the tracker's Nov. 3 entry[^27].

**What offline signals add.** On Oct. 20 ten residents spoke, and the minutes say the public voice "warned against the Data Centers." The minutes do not name them, and I found no coverage that does[^2]. Directors discussed economic development, with some wanting standards before applications arrive and others worried about limiting economic opportunity[^2]. The strongest case from the other side: Rep. Randy Torres, Chamber of Commerce president and CEO and a member of the state Joint Energy Committee, said at the Oct. 6 meeting that he would tell the board when that committee meets[^1]. Directors Burns and Carroll voted no[^2]. I found no organized group, petition or local poll. A Talk Business & Politics/Hendrix College poll in August 2026 of 2nd Congressional District likely voters found more than half strongly opposed to a data center in their community; I did not verify Benton County is in that district[^28]. Nationally, Gallup's March 2026 survey found 71% oppose a data center in their area[^29].
