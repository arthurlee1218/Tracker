# Siloam Springs, Benton County, AR: Data Center Siting Brief

As of 2026-11-06 · Built from Data Center Watch entries and sentiment data, checked against public reporting · Not legal advice; confirm with local counsel

**Bottom line.** Siloam Springs has no data center application on record, but two developers were in talks with the city when the board began a six-month moratorium. The Board of Directors passed Ordinance 26-25 on first reading Oct. 6 (4-1) and second reading Oct. 20 (5-2)[^1][^2]. The tracker records final adoption on Nov. 3 by 5-2, but I could not confirm that from an official record[^3]. With its emergency clause, the pause would run to about early May 2027[^7]. The board that decides what comes next will be partly new: four of seven seats were on the Nov. 3 ballot, two of the five directors who voted yes did not run, and a third is in a Dec. 1 runoff[^32][^33]. The biggest legal question is whether Arkansas Act 851 of 2023, written mostly for crypto mining, stops a city from pausing or banning data centers; it has not been tested in an Arkansas court[^4]. The city owns its electric utility and buys power from the Grand River Dam Authority, and any discharge must meet Oklahoma's limits on the Illinois River downstream[^5][^34].

## 1. Legal analysis

**Local status.** Director Lesa Rissler introduced Ordinance 26-25[^7]. On Oct. 6 the board passed the first reading 4-1; Brad Burns voted no and David Allen and Mindy Hunt were absent[^1] *(single source)*. Rissler said, "This isn't a permanent moratorium, it's only six months."[^1] On Oct. 20 the second reading passed 5-2. Ken Wiles, Betsy Blair-Finn, Rissler, Allen and Hunt voted yes; Reid Carroll and Burns voted no. Allen moved and Rissler seconded[^2][^35]. The Nov. 3 agenda lists the ordinance for third reading[^8]. The tracker says it passed 5-2 that day, citing a Facebook page, "Siloam Springs Citizen's Page"[^3]. The Nov. 3 minutes are not yet posted, so the roll call and effective date are unconfirmed.

**What the ordinance does.** It bars the city from accepting or approving rezoning, site plans, building permits or other approvals for a new data center. It also bars utility agreements, special rate deals and capacity reservations "specifically for" one[^7]. Existing data centers, and any with all final approvals and permits issued before the effective date, are exempt. The pause lasts six months from the effective date, and an extension needs further board action. An emergency clause makes it effective on passage[^7]. The City Administrator, Scot Rigby (hired in April 2026), must review the Unified Development Code and utility capacity, take public input and recommend rules; any code change goes to the Planning Commission and then the board[^7][^36]. The definition has no size or megawatt threshold[^7].

**Zoning authority.** The city board adopts zoning by ordinance after a Planning Commission hearing, with notice by posted sign, mailed letters and a legal notice. Applicants must "convincingly demonstrate" six findings[^9]. The city's code has no standards for large data centers[^7]. Inside city limits the county has no zoning role.

**Incentives.** Act 548 of 2025 lowered the state sales and use tax exemption threshold for a Qualified Data Center from $500 million to $100 million within five years of construction starting, with a $1 million annual compensation requirement that can count contractors' pay. It added a "Qualified Large Data Center" tier at $2 billion and $3 million, excluded crypto mining, and moved administration to the Department of Finance and Administration, which revokes the certificate if targets are missed[^10]. There is no state property tax break; that would be a local deal. On Nov. 3 voters rejected Issue 3, which would have let the legislature create economic development districts using tax-increment financing, and approved Issue 4, up to $500 million in state bonds for water, waste and flood projects[^37]. State ratepayer scrutiny so far concerns Entergy and a Google-linked solar rider in West Memphis, which does not serve Siloam Springs[^11].

**Does Act 851 limit a local pause or ban?** Act 851 (HB1799) is titled the "Arkansas Data Centers Act of 2023." It created Ark. Code 14-1-505, which barred local rules that treat crypto mining differently from other data centers[^12]. In the 2024 fiscal session, two acts gave local governments back their power to regulate crypto mining, including noise, and created state permits for the mines[^12]. Neither act addressed other data centers. The Democrat-Gazette's local story says Act 851 bars cities from banning data centers outright but leaves zoning and operating standards to them[^1] *(single source)*.

- *The case that it limits cities.* Little Rock City Attorney Tom Carpenter said the act's title and references "could be enough for a judge" to rule against a locality that bans data centers[^4] *(single source)*. Sen. Joshua Bryant (R-Rogers), the Senate sponsor, said the intent was to prevent discrimination against both data centers and crypto mining, and that a data center ban should be left to the state[^4][^12]. Attorney General Tim Griffin's Nov. 2023 opinion said a city or county "cannot prevent a new digital asset mining operation," which concerns crypto[^4] *(single source)*.
- *The case that it does not.* John Wilkerson, general counsel of the Arkansas Municipal League, said, "I think that Act 851 dealt with crypto."[^4] Rep. Rick McClure (R-Malvern), the lead House sponsor, was unsure the act covers new data centers[^4][^12]. City attorney Heather Owens of Harrington Miller Law Firm told the board on Oct. 6 it has authority to pass the ordinance[^1] *(single source)*.
- *What is unsettled and what would settle it.* The Attorney General's office said it will not say whether Act 851 covers hyperscale data centers without a formal opinion request, and no Arkansas court has ruled[^4]. A formal opinion, a court ruling or an amendment would settle it. Ordinance Section 7 says it applies "consistently with applicable state and federal law," which hedges the question[^7]. I found no named challenger to Siloam Springs' ordinance. Elsewhere, developers sued Pulaski County on Oct. 30 over its new data center rules, which could produce the first Arkansas ruling on local data center regulation[^38].

**Citizen petitions.** I did not confirm how Arkansas's initiative and referendum rules apply to an emergency-clause ordinance, and found no petition on this one. Recall of a city director takes signatures from 35% of the votes cast for that office at the last election[^39].

**Outlook.** Sen. Fred Love's non-binding interim resolution asking for a statewide moratorium while the state writes standards was referred to committee in August and has not been voted on[^13][^40]. Love was the Democratic nominee for governor; Gov. Sarah Huckabee Sanders beat him by about 15 points on Nov. 3[^37]. Sanders has not moved to restrict data centers; spokesperson Sam Dubke cited clean water and lower costs[^13]. The Legislature that convenes in January will be less one-sided: Republicans lost their three-fourths House supermajority (about 69-29, with seats still being counted) and now need Democratic votes for most spending bills, though they keep 28 of 35 Senate seats[^37]. Democrats flipped four House seats based in Benton County[^41]. I found no 2027 data center bills filed yet.

## 2. What matters most here

**Power: a city-owned utility buying from GRDA.** Siloam Springs runs its own electric department under Director Phil Stokes, with 9,351 customers, four substations and 10 miles of 69 kV line (December 2024)[^5]. It buys wholesale power from the Grand River Dam Authority under a 1989 contract that Stokes said ends in 2042[^14]. The grid operator is Southwest Power Pool; in February 2021 an energy emergency led to rotating outages of about 45 minutes each[^15]. GRDA's wholesale cost increases already reach customers: in 2025 the city passed through a 4% GRDA increase as a power cost adjustment[^42]. Housing growth is straining the system too; the utility fund borrowed nearly $3 million from the general fund to buy transformers for about 1,200 planned homes[^43]. I could not find peak load, spare capacity or a large-load tariff.

**Wastewater: upgrades, not a capacity shortage.** The wastewater plant is designed for 5.3 million gallons a day and discharges to Sager Creek[^17]. The finance director, Christina Petriches, said the plant is not facing capacity problems; the work is needed to meet updated environmental requirements, and some larger pipes are needed for growth[^18]. The Nov. 3 ballot asked voters to replace an expiring 0.625% sales tax with a new one at the same rate to repay up to $55 million in wastewater bonds; Public Works Director Josh Napier is the city contact. I did not find the result[^18]. The state environmental agency's file shows a discharge permit renewal for the plant[^19] (I could not open the file to confirm the dates).

**Downstream: Oklahoma's river rules.** Sager Creek flows to the Illinois River, which Oklahoma protects as a scenic river with a phosphorus limit of 0.037 mg/L. Under the 1992 Supreme Court ruling in *Arkansas v. Oklahoma*, upstream Arkansas discharges must meet Oklahoma's downstream water quality standards[^34]. Any cooling-water discharge from a data center would face that test. Closed-loop or air cooling avoids most of it.

**Water supply.** Raw water comes from the Illinois River in Oklahoma, stored in three reservoirs; the system serves about 18,000 people[^20]. A roughly $30 million plant and intake upgrade was finished in August 2022[^21]. I could not find the plant's post-upgrade capacity. Mavria said it would use closed-loop cooling and bring its own water to start, but did not know its total need[^1] *(single source)*.

**Rates already rising.** The board approved a rate ordinance 6-0 on Dec. 16, 2025[^22]. Per the Democrat-Gazette, electric rates stay flat in 2026 while water and wastewater rise 4%, followed by 4% a year for each through 2030, about $8 more a month for the average resident in 2026[^22] *(single source)*. Rates are the second most common concern in local posts (see section 4).

## 4. Public sentiment

**What the data shows.** The tracker holds 65 saved posts for Siloam Springs, all dated Oct. 6 to Nov. 4, 2026 (the dataset covers May 1 onward): 64 Facebook comments and one council-minute entry. By stance, 53 are against, 6 supportive, 4 mixed and 2 neutral. These are people who post online, not residents, and engagement shows reach, not agreement[^3]. Statewide the tracker holds 274 Arkansas posts, 247 against[^3].

**The main concerns, explained.**

- *Water (16 posts: 15 against, 1 mixed).* Commenters worry about supply and contamination. One comment in a Facebook group on Oct. 19 asked about "water usage and contamination," with 30 reactions and 5 replies[^25]. This connects to the unknown water need of the two proposals and to the Oklahoma downstream rule.
- *Rates (9 posts: 8 against, 1 mixed).* A comment on Oct. 19 warned that costs will rise "if you think stuff is expensive now"[^25]. This sits beside the scheduled 4% yearly water and wastewater increases. The ordinance bars special rate deals for data centers during the pause[^7][^22].
- *Noise (8 posts, all against).* The ordinance lists noise and backup generators among the topics the city must review[^7].
- *Tech and land (7 each).* Land has one supportive post. Six more posts focus on the pause itself, five of them against, such as the comment "Six-month moratorium isn't long enough."[^26]

**Voices on each side.** The tracker does not store commenter names, and I do not give names or look anyone up.

- Against: a comment, "I'd rather have a Costco than a data center," on the Arkansas Democrat-Gazette's Facebook page, Oct. 10, 2026 (45 reactions, 2 replies, top 4% on its platform)[^26]. A comment on the same thread, "Six-month moratorium isn't long enough," had 34 reactions[^26]. A water-contamination comment in a Facebook group, Oct. 19, had 30 reactions and 5 replies[^25].
- Supportive: "Everyone who reacted on this used a data center," Oct. 10, on the Democrat-Gazette's page (6 reactions, 9 replies)[^26]. A jobs comment in the Oct. 19 group thread said it would bring jobs (1 reaction, 3 replies)[^25].
- Mixed: a comment that any project "should be capable of co-generating the power it requires" (5 reactions), posted Oct. 6 on the same Facebook page the tracker cites for the Nov. 3 vote[^27].

**What offline signals add.** On Oct. 6, Utility Commission vice chair Bernadette Keck spoke in support of the moratorium, citing water use, noise and rolling blackouts[^44]. On Oct. 20 ten residents spoke, and the minutes say the public voice "warned against the Data Centers." The minutes do not name them[^2]. Directors discussed economic development, with some wanting standards before applications arrive and others worried about limiting economic opportunity[^2]. The strongest voice on the other side is Rep. Randy Torres (R-Siloam Springs), president and CEO of the Chamber of Commerce and a member of the Legislature's Joint Energy Committee, who said at the Oct. 6 meeting that he would tell the board when that committee meets[^1][^45]. Directors Burns and Carroll voted no[^2]. I found no organized group, petition or local poll. Nationally, Gallup's March 2026 survey found 71% oppose an AI data center in their area, 48% strongly[^29].

**The election.** Rissler, who introduced the moratorium, kept her Ward 4 seat 480-365 over Meghan Feyerabend[^32]. Directors Hunt (Ward 1) and Blair-Finn (Ward 3), both yes votes, were not on the ballot. Ward 1 goes to a Dec. 1 runoff between Dexter Mullin (42%) and Amy Smith (38%). Ward 2's Ken Wiles, another yes vote, led with 45% and faces Bryan McKenzie (35%) in the runoff. In Ward 3, Kevin Williams leads Mark Long 525-524 with three overseas ballots outstanding; certification is due the following week and a recount is possible[^32][^33]. I found no reporting on these candidates' views on data centers. Countywide, Democrats won four of 15 Benton County Quorum Court seats, ending all-Republican control[^41].

- A court injunction against a data center moratorium in "Benton County" concerns Benton County, Missouri, not this place[^31].
