# Siloam Springs, Benton County, AR: Data Center Siting Brief

As of 2026-11-06 · Built from Data Center Watch entries and sentiment data, checked against public reporting · Not legal advice; confirm with local counsel

**Bottom line.** The Siloam Springs Board of Directors has moved a six-month data center moratorium (Ordinance 26-25) through three readings: first reading Oct. 6, second reading Oct. 20, and, per the tracker's record, final adoption Nov. 3 on a 5-2 vote.[^1][^2][^3] I could not find independent news coverage or the ordinance text for the final vote, so the adoption rests on a director's Facebook post logged by the tracker.[^4] The biggest legal question is whether Arkansas Act 851 of 2023, written mostly for crypto mining, limits a city's power to pause or ban data centers; the city's own attorney says outright bans are barred but zoning and operating standards are allowed.[^1][^5] Two developers, Kratos AI (for an unnamed client wanting 20 MW, possibly 50 MW) and Mavria (10 MW), had been in talks with city staff and were waiting on the moratorium outcome.[^1] Over the next six months the city says it will write zoning and operating rules, which is the next real decision point.[^1]

## 1. Legal analysis

**Local status.** Oct. 6, 2026: the board approved first reading 4-1. Director Brad Burns voted no; directors David Allen and Mindy Hunt were absent. Director Lesa Rissler introduced the ordinance.[^1] Oct. 20: after ten residents spoke, all warning against data centers, the board passed second reading of Ordinance 26-25 on a 5-2 roll call; some of the two "no" directors worried about limiting economic opportunity.[^3] Nov. 3: the board adopted the six-month moratorium 5-2, and the requesting director said Arkansas law limits what a city can prohibit and urged residents to contact state legislators.[^4] I could not open the minutes, so the names of the "no" voters on Oct. 20 and Nov. 3 and of the ten speakers are not confirmed, and I could not confirm the ordinance's exact end date or exemptions. A search for November coverage found none.[^2]

**Zoning authority.** Siloam Springs zones inside its own limits through the board and planning process. The reporting describes the tools as zoning, planning commission approval and operating standards.[^1] A Benton County ordinance on data centers for unincorporated areas was reported in 2023, but I did not open it and could not confirm its content; county rules would not cover land inside the city.

**Citizen initiatives.** I could not confirm Arkansas's initiative and referendum rules for city ordinances, and found no petition on this subject.

**Water, incentives and disclosure.** I found no state data-center incentive, large-load or disclosure rule that I opened. Little Rock's June 2026 ordinance is a nearby model: Mayor Frank Scott Jr. said Act 851 prevents cities from banning data centers but lets them regulate them.[^6]

**Outlook.** Lawmakers may revisit the issue; Rep. Randy Torres, who is also the Siloam Springs Chamber of Commerce president and CEO, told the board he sits on the state Joint Energy Committee and would pass on its meeting date, which had not been set.[^1]

**Does Act 851 limit a city's data center moratorium?**
*What the law says.* Act 851 of 2023 (HB1799, "Arkansas Data Centers Act of 2023") focuses mainly on digital asset mining. Localities cannot rezone with the intent or effect of discriminating against a mining business, and such businesses may operate in industrial zones unless the area is designated for other uses.[^5] Attorney General Tim Griffin's November 2023 opinion said a city or county cannot prevent a new mining operation by ordinance, and his office would not say whether the act covers hyperscale data centers without a formal opinion request.[^5]
*The case that it limits cities.* Little Rock City Attorney Tom Carpenter said the act's title and data center references could lead a judge to rule against a locality, and that a court answer could take about three years.[^5] Mayor Scott took the same practical view.[^6] Siloam Springs' own counsel, Heather Owens of Harrington Miller Law Firm (Springdale), told the board it may pass the ordinance but described the act as barring outright bans.[^1]
*The case that it does not.* John Wilkerson, general counsel and legislative director of the Arkansas Municipal League, called the law murky and said it does not apply to data centers the way it does to crypto mining. Lead sponsor Rep. Rick McClure (R-Malvern) said he was unsure it applies to new data center facilities.[^5] Sen. Josh Bryant (R-Rogers), a co-sponsor, said banning data centers should be left to the state while locals can regulate surrounding issues.[^5]
*What would settle it.* A formal attorney general opinion on hyperscale data centers, a court ruling, or a 2027 amendment. I could not confirm whether the AG's reported 2025 statement on county moratoriums covers cities.

## 2. What matters most here

**Power comes from a city utility and a Oklahoma wholesaler.** Siloam Springs runs its own electric department, with 9,351 customers as of December 2024, four substations and 10 miles of 69 kV transmission; the director is Phil Stokes.[^7] It buys wholesale power from the Grand River Dam Authority of Oklahoma under a contract signed in 1989 that runs to 2042.[^8] A 10 to 50 MW request would be a large addition to that system, but I could not confirm the city's peak demand, a large-load tariff, or whether the contract could carry such a load.

**Wastewater is the capacity question raised by officials.** The city's wastewater plant has a design capacity of 5.3 million gallons a day and discharges treated effluent to Sager Creek; industrial dischargers need a city permit.[^9] Utility Commission member Bernadette Keck raised concern about upgrades a data center would require at the plant.[^1] I could not confirm current permit limits or how much water or sewer capacity a data center would use.

**Water supply and a closed-loop claim.** The city says it draws water from the Illinois River in Oklahoma and treats it at its own plant for about 18,000 people.[^10] Mavria's Joe Hegendeffer described a closed-loop system and said the company would bring its own water at the start, but did not know how much it would need.[^1] Plant capacity in gallons per day is not on the city page.

**Rates and a pass-through cost.** City electric rates carry a power cost adjustment that recovers GRDA's fuel and purchased-power costs; in 2022 it averaged $0.03647 per kWh versus $0.0243 in 2021.[^11] That is 2022 data, but it shows that wholesale cost swings reach customers. Statewide, lawmakers questioned Entergy Arkansas in September 2026 over charging ratepayers for solar tied to Google's West Memphis campus, a different utility and place.[^12]

## 4. Public sentiment

**What the data shows.** The tracker holds 65 saved posts for Siloam Springs from May 1 to Nov. 4, 2026: 64 on Facebook and one council-minutes item. Stance: 53 against, 6 supportive, 4 mixed, 2 neutral. These are people who post online, not residents, and engagement reflects reach, not agreement. Benton County has 66 posts, so the city is nearly all of it.[^13]

**The main concerns, explained.**
*Water (16 posts; 15 against, 0 supportive, 1 mixed).* Commenters worry about use and contamination; one group comment asked "what about the water usage and contamination?"[^14] This sits against Mavria's closed-loop statement, which came with no volume figure.[^1]
*Rates (9; 8 against, 1 mixed).* The most-engaged comment, 45 reactions and 2 replies, said "I'd rather have a Costco than a data center."[^15] Another said costs will rise once a data center is built.[^16] No utility analysis I opened says either way.
*Noise (8, all against) and tech (7, all against).* Posts on these topics are against; no source I opened addresses noise rules for Siloam Springs.
*Pause and land (6 and 7).* One comment, 34 reactions, said "Six-month moratorium isn't long enough."[^17] This links to the director's own comment that state law limits what the city can prohibit.[^4]

**Voices on each side.** The saved file stores no commenter names, and the post pages did not load for me, so for every post below the account name is not visible without logging in. All are individual commenters, not groups, outlets or officials.
- Against: the Costco comment (Oct. 10, Democrat-Gazette Facebook thread, 45/2, top 4% on its platform)[^15]; a comment that this is "how you stop a data center" from coming to a city (same thread, 45/2)[^15]; the water and contamination comment (Oct. 19, local Facebook group, 30/5).[^14]
- Supportive: a comment that everyone who reacted used a data center (Oct. 10, 6/9)[^15]; "Just build it & tax" it heavily and use the money for residents (Oct. 10, 9/6)[^15]; a group comment that it will bring jobs and that zoning and elected officials already provide protections (Oct. 19, 1/3).[^14] How replies read is not visible to me.
- Mixed: a call that any data center co-generate its own power (Oct. 6, on the Siloam Springs Citizen's Page post, 5/0).[^18]

**What offline signals add.** Ten residents spoke against data centers on Oct. 20, though the names are not confirmed.[^3] Against that, two directors voted no on second reading, and some worried about limiting economic opportunity.[^3] The strongest version of the other side: Rep. Torres leads the chamber of commerce and sits on the Joint Energy Committee, and developers had expressed interest, so the city is weighing jobs and tax base against the concerns above.[^1] I found no polls, candidate races or organized groups specific to Siloam Springs.

## 5. Other good-to-knows

- Little Rock approved data center rules June 2, 2026, with hyperscale defined as over 250,000 sq ft and over 50 MW, industrial zones only, and 1,000 ft from non-industrial areas, per press summaries; a possible template for Siloam Springs' rule-writing.[^6]
- The Utility Commission voted 4-0 on Sept. 24, 2026 to advise the board to revisit the city's solar buy-back policy, which may overlap with any large-load debate.[^8]
- Watch for: the ordinance text and end date, the Joint Energy Committee meeting date, zoning drafts during the six months, and any AG opinion request on Act 851.[^1]
- Another Democrat-Gazette Facebook thread (Oct. 10) and a local group thread (Oct. 19) were the main sentiment sources, so Siloam Springs sentiment is concentrated in two threads.[^13]
