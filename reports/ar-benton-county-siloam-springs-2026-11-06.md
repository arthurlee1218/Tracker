# Siloam Springs, Benton County, AR: Data Center Siting Brief

As of 2026-11-06 · Built from Data Center Watch entries and sentiment data, checked against public reporting · Not legal advice; confirm with local counsel

**Bottom line.** Siloam Springs' Board of Directors adopted a six-month data center moratorium on Nov. 3, 2026, by a 5-2 vote, according to the tracker's record of a post on the Siloam Springs Citizen's Page.[^1] The city had been in talks with two developers, Kratos AI and Mavria, about 10 to 50 MW facilities, and both said they were waiting on the ordinance.[^2] The biggest legal question is whether Arkansas Act 851 of 2023, written mainly for crypto mining, limits a city's power to ban or pause data centers. No Arkansas court has ruled on it.[^3] The city attorney's firm told the board it has the authority to pass the ordinance, and the director who requested the pause told residents state law limits what a city can prohibit.[^2][^1] What decides what happens next is what rules the city writes during the six months, whether its power supplier (the Grand River Dam Authority) and its wastewater plant can carry the load, and whether the legislature changes the state law.[^4][^5] I could not confirm the exact end date of the pause or any date for the next step.

## 1. Legal analysis

**Local status.** Ordinance 26-25 passed first reading on Tuesday, Oct. 6, 2026, 4-1. Director Brad Burns voted no. Directors David Allen and Mindy Hunt were absent. Director Lesa Rissler introduced it and said it is temporary ("only six months") and gives time to write local rules. The news story does not name the four yes votes.[^2] The second reading passed 5-2 on Oct. 20, after ten residents spoke, all warning against data centers. Two directors voted no, and some worried about limiting economic opportunity.[^6] The final vote on Nov. 3 was 5-2 per the tracker's record. The director who requested the ordinance said Arkansas law limits what a city can prohibit and urged residents to contact state legislators.[^1] I could not open the city's agenda packet (a file over 10 MB) or the Facebook post, and I found no news report of the final vote. I could not confirm which directors voted yes or no on Oct. 20 or Nov. 3, or the names of the ten residents who spoke.

**Zoning authority.** Attorney Heather Owens of the Harrington Miller Law Firm told the board that, under Act 851, cities cannot ban data centers outright but can regulate them through zoning, planning approvals and operating standards.[^2] I could not find the city's zoning code for data centers or the text of Ordinance 26-25.

**Citizen initiatives.** I could not confirm a municipal referendum or initiative rule from a page I opened. Search results pointed to Amendment 7 of the Arkansas Constitution, with petitions filed 30 to 90 days after passage, but I did not open the text and could not confirm the signature threshold for a city measure. No referendum petition on this ordinance has been reported that I could find.

**Water, incentives and disclosure.** Act 548 of 2025 cut the minimum investment for a sales and use tax-exempt "qualified data center" from $500 million to $100 million, with annual compensation of at least $1 million for two calendar years after operations begin. It also created a "qualified large data center" tier. The Department of Finance and Administration now takes applications. These details come from a law-firm summary, not the statute.[^7] I could not confirm any state water, disclosure or large-load rules for data centers.

**Outlook.** Sen. Fred Love (D-Little Rock) filed a non-binding interim resolution encouraging a moratorium. It had been referred to the Senate and House Insurance and Commerce committees and had not been voted on as of Aug. 24, 2026. Gov. Sarah Huckabee Sanders has declined to restrict data centers; spokesperson Sam Dubke said she would work on "common sense solutions to lower costs for Arkansans."[^8] I found no filed 2027 bill.

**Does Act 851 limit a local ban or pause?** Act 851 of 2023 (HB1799) is titled "To create the Arkansas Data Centers Act of 2023" and clarifies regulation of the digital asset mining business.[^9] It bars localities from rezoning with the intent or effect of discriminating against a digital asset mining business, and allows such businesses in industrial zones unless the area is designated for other uses.[^3] I could not read the act's body text.

- *The case that it limits local government.* Attorney General Tim Griffin's November 2023 opinion said a city or county cannot block a new crypto mining operation by ban ordinance. His spokesperson said the office would not say whether the act covers hyperscale data centers unless a formal opinion is requested.[^3] Tom Carpenter, Little Rock's city attorney, said the act's title and its data center references could lead a judge to rule against a local ban, and that litigation could take years.[^3] Sen. Josh Bryant (R-Rogers, a co-sponsor; Rogers is in Benton County) said the intent was to prevent discrimination against both data centers and crypto mining, and that banning is for the state while localities may regulate.[^3]
- *The case that it does not.* John Wilkerson, general counsel and legislative director of the Arkansas Municipal League, says the language is murky and does not apply to data centers as it does to crypto mining. Rep. Rick McClure (R-Malvern), the lead sponsor, was unsure it covers new data center facilities.[^3]
- *What remains unsettled.* As of June 7, 2026, no Arkansas court had ruled.[^3] Whether a six-month pause (as opposed to a ban) falls under the limit is a narrower question: the requesting director's statement points one way and the city attorney's firm another, and I found no written opinion on it.[^1][^2]
- *What would settle it.* A court ruling, a formal Attorney General opinion on data centers (the office says it needs a request), or an amendment to the act.[^3]

## 2. What matters most here

**The power supplier is in Oklahoma, and the contract runs to 2042.** Siloam Springs owns its electric system but no generating plants. It buys power from the Grand River Dam Authority (GRDA), under a contract dating to 1989 that runs until 2042, as electric department director Phil Stokes told the Utility Commission on Sept. 24.[^4][^10] The system serves about 9,351 customers (December 2024) through four substations at 69 kV, and its annual peak is about 70 MW, in July.[^11] The city's two pages give different customer totals (9,351 and 9,835).[^11][^12] Against a 70 MW system peak, the 20 MW to 50 MW Kratos AI asked about is large.[^2] I found no large-load tariff on the city's pages, which list Small/Large Power classes only.[^11] Whether GRDA and the Southwest Power Pool can serve the load is unanswered; Kratos said it was checking whether state utilities could support it.[^2]

**Wastewater is the stated physical constraint.** Utility Commission member Bernadette Keck raised concern at the Oct. 6 meeting about wastewater plant upgrades a data center would need.[^2] The plant is designed for 5.3 million gallons a day, discharges to Sager Creek and operates under ADEQ permit AR0020273.[^13] An April 2024 story reported that a state environmental official, Bryan Leamons, said the permit could be affected by Illinois River phosphorus negotiations between Arkansas and EPA.[^14] I could not open the permit itself and could not confirm its current numeric limits.

**Water comes from the Illinois River in Oklahoma.** The city treats river water at a conventional plant, with reservoirs north of the plant holding supplemental water.[^15] A search snippet of a 2022 project page described a $30-plus million plant upgrade, 9 MGD capacity designed to expand to 12 MGD. I did not open that page and I could not find current daily use.[^16] Mavria said it plans a closed-loop water system but would initially bring in its own water and did not know the amount it needs.[^2]

**Utility bills are already rising.** On Dec. 16, 2025 the board approved a rate ordinance 6-0: no electric increase in 2026 and 4% a year from 2027 to 2030, and 4% a year for water and sewer from 2026 to 2030. Interim City Administrator Christina Petriches said the average bill would rise about $8 a month in 2026.[^17] In February 2026 a GRDA wholesale cost pass-through also exists in rates, per city notices I saw only as snippets.[^18] This context matters because rates are the second most common concern in local posts (see section 4).

## 4. Public sentiment

**What the data shows.** The saved posts for Siloam Springs run from May 1 to Nov. 4, 2026: 65 posts, of which 64 are from Facebook and one is a meeting-minutes record. By stance, 53 are against, 6 supportive, 4 mixed and 2 neutral. These are people who post online, not residents, and engagement measures reach, not agreement.[^22] Statewide, the tracker holds 274 Arkansas posts, 247 of them against.[^22]

**The main concerns, explained.**

- *Water (16 posts: 15 against, 1 mixed).* Commenters raise water use and contamination, for example "And what about the water usage and contamination?" in a Facebook group thread.[^23] I found no source that tests those claims for Siloam Springs; the city says its supply is the Illinois River and the plant has expansion room, and no project has published a water need.[^15][^2]
- *Rates (9: 8 against, 1 mixed).* The top post says "I'd rather have a Costco than a data center."[^24] Another warns prices will rise once a data center is built.[^25] No source I opened shows how a data center would affect Siloam Springs' rates, and the city already has scheduled increases through 2030.[^17]
- *Noise (8, all against) and technology concerns (7, all against).* The saved file lists these topics but I did not read the underlying posts beyond the top ones.[^22]
- *Pause length (6: 5 against).* One commenter wrote "Six-month moratorium isn't long enough."[^26] The statement points to a view that the pause should be longer.

**Voices on each side.** Account names are not stored in the saved data, and the Facebook pages did not load for me, so the account names are not visible without logging in. Every post below is from an account marked as an individual.

- *Against.* A commenter on the Arkansas Democrat-Gazette's Facebook post, Oct. 10, 2026: "I'd rather have a Costco than a data center," engagement 45 reactions and 2 replies, top 4% on the platform.[^24] A second comment on the same thread, same engagement, said "This is how you stop a data center from coming to your city." (the saved excerpt is cut off).[^27] A commenter in Facebook group 2131183733833006, Oct. 19, asked about water and contamination, engagement 30/5, top 6%.[^23] I could not find who runs that group.
- *In favor.* A commenter on the Democrat-Gazette thread, Oct. 10: "Everyone who reacted on this used a data center," engagement 6/9.[^28] Another on the same thread argued the city should "build it & tax tf out of it," engagement 9/6.[^29] A commenter in the Facebook group, Oct. 19, said the data center would bring jobs and that zoning rules and elected officials are already in place, engagement 1/3.[^30]
- *Mixed.* One commenter wrote "We all need it, just not in my backyard."[^31]

**What offline signals add.** At the Oct. 20 meeting ten residents spoke, all against; the record I had does not name them.[^6] At the Oct. 6 meeting, Randy Torres, president and CEO of the Siloam Springs Chamber of Commerce and a state representative, said he serves on the state's Joint Energy Committee and would tell the board when it meets.[^2] The strongest case on the other side, as the two no-voting directors put it, is that a pause limits economic opportunity.[^6] The Arkansas Times reported an August Talk Business & Politics and Hendrix College poll finding more than half of likely voters in the 2nd Congressional District strongly oppose a data center in their community. That article does not mention Siloam Springs and I could not confirm the district covers it.[^32] I found no poll specific to this place and no election tied to data centers here.

## 5. Other good-to-knows

- Other Arkansas places have moved on this: Madison County's Quorum Court passed a three-year moratorium in mid-August 2026, Independence County has the longest, and Fayetteville added restrictions in August 2026, including wastewater pretreatment compliance and battery storage as primary backup power.[^8] I found no moratorium in Bentonville, Rogers, Bella Vista, Centerton or Gentry.
- Do not confuse this with Benton County, Missouri, which has its own moratorium dispute with a developer. I saw that only as a search snippet.[^33]
- Little Rock's board unanimously approved data center regulations, and Pulaski County's Quorum Court failed to pass a 12-month moratorium in May 2026.[^3]
- A federal bill on charging large power users for grid upgrades failed 57-43 in the U.S. Senate on Sept. 30, 2026 (it needed 60); Sens. Tom Cotton and John Boozman voted yes.[^34]
- Dates to watch: the end of the six-month pause (I could not confirm the effective date), the meeting of the Joint Energy Committee Rep. Torres mentioned (no date found), and any request for a formal Attorney General opinion.[^2][^3]
